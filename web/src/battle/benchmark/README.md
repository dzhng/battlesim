# Battle benchmark

One recording path measures a real simulation and renderer while a fixed camera tour flies. A preset names the work; it is never a fallback for a missing map or a failed preparation.

The benchmark (`/benchmark`, or by name `?preset=city-contact`) uses the existing simulation-owned central contact stress on a complete generated world. Its opening fighters are concentrated while reserves and logistics remain at the rear. Waves command the surviving fixed roster; their schedule alone does not establish sustained combat. Its synthetic starts and seeded orders are explicit; it does not measure player deployment or replace infantry travel. The map enters through the normal map source and preparation worker, and that worker becomes the battle authority.

A report records the selected workload fingerprint and exact applied tour. Generated reports also retain the checked request, returned map identity, derived map extents and stress identity. The fight stays at the playable centre; the overview frames the rendered landscape, including its visual-only margin. The existing display environment is drawn and counted too. Compare rows only when those inputs agree; a generator, physical catalogue, margin or rule change creates a different control even if the preset name stays the same.

Leaving preparation closes its worker. Once ready, timing starts after the declared warm tick and the first drawn frame; a cancellation produces a partial result rather than a complete performance verdict. Selecting and preparing the workload is a functional proof; the full timed window remains the frame-cost gate.

## The graphics test and its capture

The graphics test (`/graphics-test`) plays the menu reel and times it. It is also the control a second client draws against: each scene's `battle-presentation-capture` records the observed publications and, for every drawn frame, the view the rig actually drew (eye, target and up in sim space, after clamping, clearance and lag), the interpolated sim time and the canvas size. Beside it, a `native-visual-presentation` manifest names the battle by the hashes of the map file and the capture file, and records the lens, the light the frames were drawn under and the layers the browser drew. It also lists the ground's and the grass's GPU tables as hashed artifacts beside it (the height pages, the terrain material's tables, and the grass pass's params, prop grid, blade shapes and index strips), exactly the words the page uploaded, each with the layout that reads them (derived from the GPU structs, so it cannot drift from them). The grass params are taken as one frame wrote them: their view fields (side planes, eye, window origin and size) belong to that frame and are recomputed from each frame record. Each plate has a row of grass counts: the clumps that frame grew, by the cell of a 4×3 screen grid their roots project into. [The capture](presentationCapture.ts) and [the manifest](visualPresentation.ts) each have one strict validator; their formats change in place, and a stale file is refused rather than migrated.

`?plates=1` reads each shot's middle frame back from the canvas in the task that drew it, so a plate is exactly what that frame record drew, without the page's HUD or the fade veil. Its grass is read back in the same task, so the counts belong to the plate's frame, and the same frame is drawn again offscreen in the frame's ground-classes view and read back as `class-<index>.png` beside `plate-<index>.png`, and drawn once more lit without its grass as `plain-<index>.png`: the same ground with no crop over its furrows, mottle and wear. Both names follow the plate's by rule, and the canvas and the next frame are untouched. Read-backs happen only with plates, and a run with plates is not a timing measure. `?scenes=` runs chosen scenes. The [`native-parity-capture` scene](../../../scenes/native-parity-capture.mjs) runs it unattended and writes each scene's files to `throwaway/native-parity/`. The browser frame is not reproducible across runs (the simulation ticks in real time), so a plate is only ever compared with the frame record from its own run.

### The ground-class plates

A class plate shows what the terrain material says the bare ground under each pixel is, as bytes rather than colour ([`groundClasses.ts`](../../../../packages/battle-renderer/src/terrain/groundClasses.ts) owns the encoding; the view skips tone mapping, so the bytes are exact). Only the bare terrain is classed: grass, water, glass and effects are not drawn, and every pixel not wholly bare terrain (a building, a unit, a prop, the sky, the map's backdrop) is black, `(0, 0, 0)`. A ground pixel's bytes are:

- **R, the paving:** `clamp(round(128 − d × 8), 1, 255)`, where `d` is how far inside the nearest paved edge the point lies in metres (negative outside). 128 is the edge, lower is on the paving; it saturates where the ground's look stops reading the distance.
- **G, the water:** the same, for the water's edge.
- **B, the field:** `forest << 6 | kind << 2 | hash`. `forest` is 0 for none, 1 for the forest floor's verge and 2 for inside a forest's shape. `kind` is the plot's kind (its index in the biome's plots, at most 15). `hash` is `pcgHash(plot index) & 3`, so neighbouring plots of one kind differ.

A pixel that straddles two classes holds a blend of their bytes (multisampling), so agreement is measured away from edges.
